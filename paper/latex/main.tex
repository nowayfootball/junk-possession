\begin{abstract}
Ball possession is the most-cited and most-misleading number in football:
sixty percent of the ball spent recycling in one's own half is not the same as
sixty percent spent pinning the opponent back. Existing possession-value
frameworks --- expected threat, VAEP, on-ball value --- refine the count by
pricing \emph{on-ball} actions, but they are blind to the off-ball question a
sterile possession actually poses: did holding the ball \emph{create space},
or was the circulation genuinely dead? We answer this in two layers. First, an
event-side \emph{junk-possession index} decomposes possession into sequences,
prices each by its counterfactual threat potential under an expected-threat
grid, and --- after reconstructing the live scoreline to exclude
lead-protecting circulation --- flags low-threat sequences in tied-or-losing
game states. On the 2026 FIFA World Cup (101 matches, 202 team-matches) this
flag correlates negatively with points ($r=-0.39$) and expected-goal
difference ($r=-0.52$); teams above 55\% possession with below-median
efficiency average 0.62 goals and 1.00 points versus 2.24 and 2.23 for
efficient dominators. Crucially, the flag is not a repackaging of on-ball
value: with team offensive VAEP and field tilt held fixed in the same
regression, the junk flag remains a strong negative predictor of points
($p<10^{-4}$) while VAEP itself is not significant --- the index carries
off-ball information that on-ball action value does not. Second, for a flagged
window we resolve \emph{why} it was junk by projecting broadcast video to pitch
coordinates and measuring a \emph{Space-Creation Index} (SCI): whether the
possession pushed the opponent's block back. Across 31 flagged windows from
nine World Cup matches, 74\% are spatially confirmed dead, 19\% weak
progression, and 6\% genuine space creation that the event flag alone would
misclassify --- including a possessing team that dominated 73\% of the ball and
went out on penalties (two windows, both spatially dead) set against an
unlucky window that created space without a shot. The two layers together
separate ``unlucky'' from ``sterile'' possession, a distinction on-ball
possession value cannot make.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Possession percentage is quoted after every match and understood by almost
no-one. It conflates three different things a team can do with the ball ---
where it holds the ball, whether that holding threatens the goal, and in what
game state it happens --- into a single number that is frequently uncorrelated
with, and sometimes inversely related to, winning. The analytics response has
been to \emph{value} what happens on the ball: expected threat
(xT)~\citep{singh2019xt} grids the pitch by scoring potential; VAEP~\citep{decroos2019actions}
and EPV~\citep{fernandez2021epv} estimate the change in scoring/conceding
probability around each action; on-ball value~\citep{statsbomb2021obv} and
pass-risk models~\citep{power2017passes,link2016dfl} price individual
touches. These are real advances, and they are all fundamentally
\emph{on-ball}: they evaluate the decision of the player in possession.

But the question a sterile possession poses is off-ball. When a team holds 65\%
of the ball, draws 0--0, and loses on penalties, we want to know whether the
possession was \emph{unlucky} --- it dragged the opponent's block out of shape
and manufactured dangerous space that went unconverted --- or \emph{dead} ---
the block never moved and the ball simply circulated. On-ball action value
cannot tell these apart, because the distinguishing evidence is the position of
the twenty-one players \emph{without} the ball: whether the defensive block was
compressed and displaced. That evidence is spatial, and for the vast majority
of matches --- outside the handful of leagues with commercial tracking --- the
only available source of it is the broadcast video.

This paper contributes an off-ball possession-quality framework in two layers:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An event-side junk-possession index} (Section~\ref{sec:index})
that flags low-threat possession in decision-relevant game states, and a
validation battery (Section~\ref{sec:validation}) showing it predicts
outcomes, survives out-of-sample tests, and --- our central claim --- is
\emph{orthogonal} to on-ball possession value: controlling for VAEP does not
remove its predictive content.
\item \textbf{A spatial Space-Creation Index} (Section~\ref{sec:sci}) computed
from broadcast video via a game-state-reconstruction (GSR) pipeline, which
adjudicates \emph{why} a flagged possession was junk --- space-creating or
dead --- and a tournament-scale study of 31 flagged windows from nine World Cup
matches (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

The event layer scans whole matches cheaply and plants time-stamped flags; the
spatial layer answers ``why'' on the short broadcast windows the flags point
to. Neither layer alone suffices: event data cannot see the block, and
processing 90 minutes of broadcast video per match to pitch coordinates is
neither necessary nor, on commodity hardware, cheap. Our position is that the
macro/micro split is the practical way to bring off-ball spatial evidence to
possession analysis at the scale of a whole tournament.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Possession value.} Expected threat~\citep{singh2019xt} assigns each
pitch location a scoring potential and values ball progression between
locations. VAEP~\citep{decroos2019actions} and its EPV
predecessors~\citep{fernandez2021epv} learn, from event or tracking data, the
change in scoring and conceding probability that each action induces, thereby
valuing defensive actions, failed actions, and risk. On-ball
value~\citep{statsbomb2021obv} is a commercial analogue. All price the action
of the player on the ball; none model whether a possession reshaped the
off-ball defensive block. Our index is complementary: Section~\ref{sec:vaep}
shows empirically that it captures outcome-relevant variance VAEP does not.

\paragraph{Sterile possession and territory.} ``Sterile domination'' and
``field tilt'' (share of final-third touches) are established descriptive
concepts in the analytics community. We show (Section~\ref{sec:partial}) that
field tilt explains expected goals but not points, and that the junk flag
carries points-relevant information beyond tilt --- possession quality and
territory are different axes.

\paragraph{Pitch control and space.} Pitch-control
models~\citep{spearman2017physics,spearman2018beyond} estimate, from player
positions, which team controls each point of the pitch; they require the
positions of all players. The Space-Creation Index is a differential
pitch-control statistic. Because broadcast video shows only a subset of
players, computing it from video invokes the off-screen-imputation problem
studied in our companion work; here we use the same CPU-only GSR pipeline and
report windows where the spatial verdict is robust to that imputation.

\paragraph{Broadcast GSR.} Turning broadcast footage into pitch coordinates
combines camera calibration~\citep{gutierrez2024pnlcalib,falaleev2024keypoints},
multi-object tracking~\citep{aharon2022botsort}, and team assignment, evaluated
by GS-HOTA~\citep{somers2024soccernet}. Trajectory-imputation methods for the
players a camera or sensor misses~\citep{omidshafiei2022,everett2023inferring,
capellera2024transportmer} are learned and typically bidirectional; our
pipeline is training-free and online. We consume GSR output as a metric
substrate rather than advancing GSR itself.

\section{The Junk-Possession Index}
\label{sec:index}

\paragraph{Sequences and sequence value.} We segment each match into possession
sequences: maximal runs of consecutive events by one team. Each sequence $s$ is
priced by
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{value}(s) = \underbrace{\max(0,\ \mathrm{xT}_{\max}(s) - \mathrm{xT}_{\mathrm{start}}(s))}_{\text{(i) counterfactual threat potential}}
+ 0.7\,\underbrace{\mathrm{xG}(s)}_{\text{(ii) realised shot}}
+ 0.10\,\underbrace{\mathbb{1}[\text{box entry}]}_{\text{(iii) penalty-box entry}},
\label{eq:value}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{xT}$ is a Karun-Singh-style expected-threat grid evaluated at
each touch location. Term (i) is the design core: a sequence is credited for
the \emph{peak} threat location it reached even if no shot resulted, so a
possession that worked the ball into a dangerous position but did not finish is
not scored as worthless. Term (ii) is the quality of the shot the sequence
produced, if any; because the event feed's shot-xG field is a constant
placeholder, we substitute our own gradient-boosted xG model evaluated on shot
location (body part and phase approximated). Term (iii) rewards penalty-box
entry. Sequence values are normalised by the corpus-wide 90th percentile to
$q(s) \in [0,1]$; a sequence with $q < 0.15$ is a \emph{junk sequence} ---
sustained possession of negligible threat.

\paragraph{Game-state normalisation.} Circulating in one's own half to protect
a lead is sound tactics, not junk. We reconstruct the live scoreline from goal
events and tag each sequence with the possessing team's goal difference at its
start. The headline metric \junk{} counts junk-sequence share only in
\emph{tied-or-losing} states ($\text{state} \le 0$) --- circulation with no
excuse. Team-level aggregates (Table~\ref{tab:metrics}) follow: raw possession,
threat-weighted \emph{effective} possession, efficiency
$\eff = \sum_s n_s q_s / \sum_s n_s$, \junk{}, field tilt, and a sterile index.

\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule

\midrule
Raw\% & Share of on-ball events \\
Effective\% & Threat-weighted possession share, $\sum n_s q_s$ \\

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

We validate on all 202 team-match rows of the 2026 FIFA World Cup group and
knockout stages (101 matches). Knockout draws decided on penalties are scored
by their shootout result (advance $=$ win), not as draws.

\subsection{Does junk possession relate to outcomes?}
\label{sec:corr}

Table~\ref{tab:corr} gives Pearson correlations of each possession metric with
match outcomes. \junk{} is negatively associated with expected goals
($-0.38$), goals ($-0.37$), points ($-0.39$), and xG difference ($-0.52$):
the more a team circulates without threat in a tied-or-losing state, the less
it actually creates and wins. Threat-weighting helps --- effective possession
tracks xG better than raw possession ($0.55$ vs $0.49$) --- and efficiency is
the cleanest threat signal ($0.59$). Field tilt explains xG well ($0.59$) but
points poorly ($0.19$): camping in the final third without winning is precisely
what junk captures and tilt does not (Section~\ref{sec:partial}).

Splitting the 70 teams that held more than 55\% possession by efficiency
sharpens the point: the 8 below-median (junk) dominators averaged 1.07 xG,
0.62 goals, and 1.00 points; the 62 efficient dominators averaged 2.05 xG,
2.24 goals, and 2.23 points. Holding the ball without efficiency yields 28\% of
the goals and draw-level points (Figure~\ref{fig:quadrant}).

\subsection{Learned sequence weights}
A logistic model predicting whether a possession sequence ends in a shot, from
its peak threat gain, box entry, final-third share and touch count, reaches
5-fold AUC $0.907 \pm 0.004$. All threat features load positively; box entry
was under-weighted by the initial heuristic ($0.03 \to 0.10$, adopted in
Eq.~\ref{eq:value}). Final-third share is deliberately \emph{excluded} from
Eq.~\ref{eq:value}: including it collapses the index toward field tilt, which
Section~\ref{sec:partial} shows does not predict points.

\subsection{Not a repackaging of territory}
\label{sec:partial}
Regressing points on raw possession, field tilt, and \junk{} jointly, \junk{}
remains a significant negative predictor ($\beta=-0.023$, $p=0.008$) while
field tilt does not ($p=0.19$); regressing xG difference, the sign flips ---
tilt is strongly significant ($p<10^{-4}$) and \junk{} is absorbed
($p=0.51$). ``Did the team create chances'' is answered by territory; ``did it
win'' is answered by possession quality. The two are different axes, not a
relabelling.

\subsection{Orthogonality to on-ball possession value}
\label{sec:vaep}
The sharpest objection is that the junk index merely repackages on-ball
possession value (EPV/VAEP/OBV). We test this directly with our own VAEP
implementation~\citep{decroos2019actions} trained on the same event schema. At
the surface, team offensive VAEP is \emph{correlated} with the possession-quality
metrics ($r=-0.49$ with \junk{}, $+0.58$ with efficiency, $+0.52$ with
effective possession) --- so the metrics are not orthogonal by construction;
VAEP is a genuine competitor. Yet when VAEP and field tilt are entered as
controls, the possession-quality metrics dominate the outcome variance and VAEP
adds nothing significant (Table~\ref{tab:vaep}): \junk{} predicts points at
$p<10^{-4}$ with VAEP not significant ($p=0.40$), and efficiency predicts xG
difference at $p<10^{-4}$ with VAEP not significant ($p=0.46$). A surface
correlation of $\approx0.5$ collapses to nothing once both terms share a
regression --- the junk index carries outcome-relevant, off-ball information
that on-ball action value does not. This is the paper's central quantitative
claim.

\subsection{Out-of-sample}
In-sample correlations risk tautology (describing games already lost). Two
splits that never use the target game or period: (a) \emph{first-to-second
half} --- a team's first-half efficiency predicts its second-half xG
($r=+0.33$) and first-half \junk{} predicts lower second-half xG ($r=-0.25$),
with team strength implicitly controlled by staying within one match; (b)
\emph{leave-one-match-out team trait} --- a team's mean efficiency in its
\emph{other} matches predicts this match's points ($r=+0.31$) and its mean
\junk{} predicts lower points ($r=-0.31$) and xG difference ($-0.34$).
Junk possession is a persistent team property, not a post-hoc description.

\subsection{Dead versus set-up junk}
Extending the label across possession boundaries, a junk sequence is
\emph{redeemed} if the same team regains the ball within 25\,s and reaches
threat --- a build-up, not dead circulation. Only 9\% of tied-or-losing junk is
redeemed; 91\% is genuinely dead. The dead-only variant improves in-sample
prediction slightly (points $r$: $-0.39 \to -0.42$) but is essentially
identical out-of-sample --- an honest null: most flagged junk really is dead,
so the refinement adds interpretation, not power.

\section{The Spatial Layer: Space-Creation Index}
\label{sec:sci}

The event index sees only the ball; it flags \emph{that} a possession failed to
threaten, not \emph{why}. To adjudicate whether a flagged possession created
space, we project broadcast video to pitch coordinates and measure the change
in pitch control.

\paragraph{GSR pipeline.} We use a CPU-only broadcast pipeline of open
components: PnLCalib camera calibration~\citep{gutierrez2024pnlcalib} with a
temporal anchor bridge, BoT-SORT tracking~\citep{aharon2022botsort},
colour-based team assignment (per-match Lab chroma comparators, robust to
daylight), and shot-change gating to drop replays and close-ups. Off-screen
players are imputed with a training-free ghosting layer (companion work); we
report windows where the SCI verdict is robust to that imputation.

\paragraph{Space-Creation Index.} For a flagged window with possessing team
$P$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SCI} = \Delta_{\mathrm{own}} + \Delta_{\mathrm{opp}},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_{\mathrm{own}}$ is the change in $P$'s pitch-control share of the
attacking third between the first and last thirds of the window, and
$\Delta_{\mathrm{opp}}$ is the collapse of the opponent's control share in its
own advanced zone (both percentage points, from the pitch-control model of
\citet{spearman2017physics}). Large SCI $=$ the possession pushed the block
back and seized dangerous space (space creation, unlucky); SCI $\approx 0$ $=$
the block never moved (dead junk, spatially confirming the event flag). We use
the prespecified operational bins from earlier internal work: \emph{space
creation} (SCI $\ge +12$), \emph{weak progression} ($+4 \le$ SCI $< +12$),
\emph{dead possession} (SCI $< +4$).

\paragraph{Macro/micro roles.} The two layers divide labour
(Table~\ref{tab:layers}): the event index scans whole matches instantly and
plants time-stamped flags; the spatial layer processes the short broadcast
window each flag points to and answers why.

\caption{The macro (event) and micro (spatial) layers.}

\section{Tournament-Scale Results}
\label{sec:results}

We processed every event-flagged junk window (auto-clipped from full-match
video by scoreboard-clock kickoff alignment, extra time excluded) for nine 2026
World Cup matches: 31 windows survived the pipeline
(Table~\ref{tab:agg}). Across the tournament, \textbf{74\% of flagged junk is
spatially confirmed dead}, 19\% weak progression, and \textbf{6\% genuine space
creation} that the event flag alone would misclassify as failure. The
distribution is stable as the corpus grew from six matches (21 windows;
71/24/5) to nine (31; 74/19/6): most junk really is dead, and the spatial layer
earns its keep on the minority the event flag gets wrong.

\paragraph{Case: sterile domination that went out.} Germany held 73\% of the
ball against Paraguay in the Round of 16, drew 1--1, and were eliminated on
penalties. Both flagged Germany windows are spatially dead (SCI $-24.4$ and
$-14.2$): the ball moved, the Paraguayan block did not. This is the textbook
the index is built to catch --- a scoreline (elimination) that possession
percentage alone would never predict from a dominant side, confirmed
off-ball. Correctly scoring the shootout as a Germany loss (not a draw)
matters: treating penalty eliminations as draws is a common miscoding that
would blunt exactly this signal.

\paragraph{Case: unlucky, not sterile.} In the same match, a Paraguay window at
76:01 scores SCI $+18.0$ --- space creation. The event index flagged it as
junk (no shot), but Paraguay dragged the block out of shape and manufactured
space it did not convert. This is the 6\% the two-layer design exists for: the
event flag and the spatial verdict disagree, and the spatial layer is right.
Figure~\ref{fig:sci} contrasts this window with a dead Germany window from the
same match: the possessing team's control of the attacking third surges
($+28$\pp{}) when it creates space, and falls ($-11$\pp{}) while the opponent's
block advances ($+13$\pp{}) when the possession is dead.

\paragraph{Case: junk flags on a winning side.} Norway beat Brazil despite a
high junk-flag rate; all four flagged Norway windows are spatially dead. The
flags were correct --- that possession \emph{was} dead --- and the win came
from the efficient, non-junk moments the index does not flag. High junk share
and a win are not a contradiction; they are a team that wasted much of the ball
and still converted enough.

\paragraph{Event-side contrast.} The event layer already separates cases that
share a scoreline. South Africa lost 0--1 to Canada holding 57\% (efficiency
0.27, \junk{} 57\%, tied until the 91st minute): textbook junk, no excuse.
South Korea also held 57\% and lost 1--0 to Mexico, but at efficiency 0.41 ---
they used the ball and lost to finishing, not sterility. Same possession, same
result, opposite diagnosis.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

The event index is a ball-only approximation of an off-ball question; the
spatial layer supplies the off-ball evidence only on short flagged windows, not
whole matches --- a macro/micro hybrid, which we argue is the realistic optimum
for broadcast footage but is not full-match tracking. The xG term uses shot
location only (body part and phase approximated), and the sequence weights
(0.7, 0.10) and thresholds ($q<0.15$; SCI $+12/+4$) are round operational
values, partly justified by the learned weights (Section~\ref{sec:validation})
but not externally optimised. On the spatial side, team assignment degrades for
low-contrast kits (white and striped kits, night matches): three windows were
excluded on this ground and one on clip quality, and per-match kit comparators
were set by inspection. Extra-time windows are excluded because regulation
kickoff offsets do not transfer. The SCI depends on pitch control computed over
imputed off-screen players; we report only windows robust to the imputation,
but a systematic sensitivity study across the full corpus is future work, as is
velocity-aware space \emph{value} (weighting controlled space by threat) and a
learned replacement for the operational thresholds.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

The event-side index, validation battery (including the VAEP orthogonality
regression), and tournament aggregation run on CPU over an event corpus and are
released with code at \url{https://github.com/nowayfootball/junk-possession}.
The spatial case-study pipeline consumes World Cup broadcast footage we cannot
redistribute; the pipeline code is described but the footage and per-clip
artifacts are not included. No GPU or cloud expenditure was used.
